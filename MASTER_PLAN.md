## Purpose and definition of success

Develop a physically defensible, computationally practical model that can reveal **meaningful trends in microplastic transport as water flow and particle shape change**. The primary objective is to establish the direction, relative importance and mechanisms of these effects within a stated domain. Exact field-scale escape counts or highly precise release percentages are not the target.

The motivating comparisons are:

- With the same ten spherical particles represented in the same sand matrix, how does changing convective water flow affect downstream export, travel and contact dwell?
- At the same imposed water flow, does a matched population of spheres or fibres show greater downstream export, and through what mechanism?
- Does the sphere–fibre difference depend on flow intensity? Can the ordering weaken, strengthen or reverse?

The intended contribution is evidence that future transport models should represent microplastic shape appropriately **where shape changes the predicted response**. This is a hypothesis to test, not a conclusion to impose. A robust absence of a detectable shape effect in part of the tested domain is also useful: it identifies where a spherical approximation may be adequate.

**Success means a reproducible contrast that survives numerical, sampling and relevant model-assumption checks.** A smooth curve, ten illustrative trajectories, or a preferred shape ranking alone is insufficient. Absolute predictions may have bias, but shape-dependent or flow-dependent bias cannot be assumed to cancel in a comparison.

The project has two connected workstreams:

| Workstream | Governing question | Main deliverable |
|---|---|---|
| **Part I — Research plan** | What comparison and evidence would establish a meaningful flow or shape trend? | A controlled study, uncertainty assessment and bounded mechanistic conclusion |
| **Part II — Numerical work plan** | What is the least costly trustworthy implementation capable of answering that comparison? | A verified solver, affordable execution and reproducible trend analysis |

## R1. Freeze the research questions and claim boundary

| Question | Primary contrast | Supporting explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Flow effect within each shape | Change in exported fraction between low, reference and high flow | Advection relative to settling, contact dwell and pore passage |
| Shape effect at each flow | Fibre exported fraction minus sphere exported fraction | Orientation, anisotropic hydrodynamic response and geometric obstruction |
| Shape × flow interaction | Change in the shape contrast between flow levels | Flow-dependent alignment, contact engagement or release from contacts |

Do not assume that stronger flow always produces greater export, or that fibres always escape less than spheres. Distinguish a monotonic trend, a transition, a reversal and an unresolved effect.

**Primary scope:** dilute microplastics in a saturated, fixed sand matrix under prescribed steady water flow. Start with equal-volume spheres and rigid AR4 prolates as fibre surrogates, using the existing material scenario. A rigid prolate does not represent bending, entanglement, fraying or every real fibre geometry.

Use the precise phrase **whole-body downstream export from a declared synthetic initial population** for the present boundary model. The existing downstream plane in periodically continued geometry is an observation boundary; it is not every external face of a laboratory column. Initially non-overlapping particles placed at zero velocity do not automatically represent a mechanically equilibrated or chemically attached resident population. Claims about detachment from prepared soil need a separate preparation/release protocol.

The earlier permeability work remains useful context and a possible later coupling study. It is no longer the primary success criterion for this phase. A one-way frozen-flow transport result does not establish time-varying clogging or permeability change.

## R2. Make sphere–fibre comparisons fair

| Control | Required treatment |
|---|---|
| Sand matrix | Same packing, porosity definition, grain geometry and boundaries within each paired comparison |

For prolate semiaxes `(a,b,b)`, the equal-volume sphere radius is `r = (a b²)^(1/3)`. Equal volume deliberately leaves different lengths and cross-sections: those geometric differences are part of this shape comparison. Equal maximum length or equal diameter answers a different question and must be a separately labelled sensitivity study.

Ten such trajectories represent a **dilute ten-particle ensemble**, not a simulation of ten mutually interacting particles. ## R3. Define water exposure before comparing outcomes

Use mean interstitial velocity `U = Q/(n A)` as the controlled flow intensity, where `Q` is discharge, `n` the stated pore-volume fraction and `A` the cross-sectional area. Record Darcy flux `Q/A` separately.

For a uniform-area observation domain of length `L`, define flushed pore volumes as:

`PV(t) = integral[Q(t) dt] / V_pore`, with `PV = U t / L` for constant flow and `V_pore = n A L`.

Report two comparisons because they answer different questions:

1. **Primary: equal flushed pore volume.** Compare export at the same water throughput. Different flow intensities then have different physical durations, so gravity and other time-dependent mechanisms can still contribute to the contrast.
2. **Companion: equal physical time.** Compare removal over the same duration. Higher flow then delivers more water; that throughput difference is part of this operational comparison.

For the same-flow shape comparison, equal PV also implies equal elapsed time. Never compare cases at whichever stopping time each happened to reach.

Retain the already declared **2-PV observation as the initial export-feasibility target**. Use its implied low-flow duration as the largest runtime challenge. Before a confirmatory matrix, freeze the common PV queries and the common-time window that all conditions will cover. A shorter development test is acceptable, but cannot silently replace the export horizon or establish a ranking from unequal observations.

## R5. Measure trends and their mechanisms

For each shape `s`, flow `U`, packing `k` and query `P`:

`R(s,U,k,P) = N_whole-body-downstream-exits-by-P / N_initial`.

For equal-volume, equal-density particles without fragmentation, this number fraction also equals the exported mass fraction. “Still present at P” is a finite-horizon observation, not permanent retention.

Primary derived quantities are:

- **Shape contrast:** `D_shape(U,P) = R(fibre,U,P) - R(sphere,U,P)`; positive means more fibre export under those conditions.
- **Flow contrast:** `D_flow(s,P) = R(s,U_high,P) - R(s,U_low,P)`; inspect the middle level before calling the response monotonic.
- **Interaction:** `I(P) = D_shape(U_high,P) - D_shape(U_low,P)`.

Use the same predeclared packing weights and paired population for these contrasts. Analyse the full export-versus-PV and export-versus-time curves as well as frozen query points.

Supporting observables should explain the contrasts: downstream travel of non-exported particles, first-passage times, finite contact-dwell distributions, orientation relative to flow, active-contact geometry, and normalized particle speed. Count actual geometric contact separately from predicted implicit contact. Average travel speed among exported particles alone can be selection-biased; retain the non-exported population in companion analyses.

If all export fractions are near zero or one, a mechanism may still be visible in travel or dwell, but that does not establish an export difference. Revise a subsequent protocol transparently if the original observation window cannot discriminate the question.

## R6. Decide what counts as a scientifically meaningful trend

Before confirmatory runs, freeze a minimum effect of interest for each primary contrast and the uncertainty needed to distinguish it. This is a study-design choice, not a fitted parameter. A possible numerical planning rule is to keep the resolution-induced change in a contrast below one third of its minimum effect of interest; justify the actual values prospectively.

A supported trend requires:

1. **Controlled comparison:** changes are attributable to the declared flow/shape factors under the chosen population and exposure definition.
2. **Numerical resolution:** sign, relevant ordering and broad contrast magnitude persist under the verification checks in N4. Numerical uncertainty is small compared with the asserted effect.
3. **Replication:** report per-packing paired contrasts and uncertainty; an overall average must not conceal a strong packing-dependent reversal.
4. **Model robustness:** plausible uncertainty in the mechanisms that could distinguish shapes does not erase or reverse the claimed conclusion, or the conclusion is narrowed to the conditions where it holds.
5. **Failure robustness:** unresolved outcomes cannot explain the apparent ranking.
6. **Physical credibility:** independent evidence exercises the relevant shape-sensitive mechanisms. Without matched validation, label the result a numerical model prediction, not an experimentally established trend.

Do not assume computational censoring is independent of shape or trapping. With `E` observed exports and `M` unresolved outcomes among `N` planned equally weighted trajectories, retain the accounting interval `[E/N, (E+M)/N]`. Include unrun selected cases in `M`. Use weights for unequal designs. These are missing-outcome bounds, not sampling confidence intervals.

For a difference between conditions A and B, conservative bounds are `[L_A - U_B, U_A - L_B]`. If the bounds permit opposite rankings, the trend is unresolved. Independently quantify sampling and numerical uncertainty. Model-domain violations additionally fail the applicability gate; missing-outcome bounds do not repair an invalid physical model.

Use three outcome labels: **supported within the tested domain**, **condition-dependent**, or **unresolved/no effect detected at the available resolution**. Do not treat lack of significance as equivalence, or select only cases that support the desired narrative.

## R7. Build the paper around the comparison

Planned figures are:

| Figure | Scientific purpose |
|---|---|
| Matrix, equal-volume shapes and boundary/initialization diagram | Make the comparison and claim domain reproducible |
| Export versus PV and time, with per-packing results and unresolved bounds | Show the flow response without hiding exposure or missing outcomes |
| Shape contrast versus flow | Show shape importance and any interaction or reversal |
| Orientation, contact dwell and travel distributions | Explain the observed difference mechanically |
| Numerical and model-assumption sensitivity of the contrasts | Demonstrate that the conclusion is not an implementation artifact |

Use conditional conclusion wording: “Within the tested geometry, particle-size ratio and flow range, shape altered [observable] through [supported mechanism], so a spherical approximation requires justification for this application.” If the shape contrast is unresolved, report that and identify the evidence needed to resolve it. Do not manufacture a “fibres always retain more” conclusion.

Relevant primary studies motivate these mechanisms without determining our result. Fouty and Engdahl's idealized porous-medium experiments found fibre travel behaviour that required attention to delays and interactions with surrounding obstacles; their setup is not a matched validation of our AR4 sand model. [Water Resources Research, 2024](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2024WR037901).

Xu and colleagues reported flow-dependent retention and redistribution in a two-dimensional saturated porous-medium experiment. This motivates testing a flow response, but does not prescribe a universal monotonic law for fibres in three-dimensional sand. [Journal of Hazardous Materials, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39933465/).
